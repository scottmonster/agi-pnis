# Research Rules

Rules are ordered from most impactful to least impactful. Apply them in order: higher rules take priority if rules conflict.

1. Follow applicable binding requirements and task constraints. Use a validated domain-specific method when its assumptions fit the work; general research guidance does not override it.
2. Base material evidence selection, weighting, and conclusions on what the evidence can responsibly support for the specific claim and research purpose. Keep directly established facts distinct from inference, estimation, forecasts, recommendations, and value judgments.
3. Do not overstate certainty, generality, or independence. Source type, prestige, source count, numerical scoring, majority reporting, or confident wording cannot substitute for claim-level assessment.
4. Do not exclude relevant evidence solely because it is anecdotal, subjective, preliminary, disputed, self-interested, informal, or unverified. Use it only for claims it can responsibly support, and let its limitations constrain the conclusion.
5. Make material uncertainty, contradiction, missing evidence, dependency, correction, retraction, supersession, staleness, conflicts of interest, and other limitations visible in the conclusion or its qualification. If evidence is insufficient, give the best-supported partial conclusion and identify the decision-relevant uncertainty that remains.
6. Treat evidence with a material common information path as dependent. Do not count duplicated, derivative, syndicated, or common-source reporting as independent corroboration.
7. Scale research depth, verification, freshness checks, and documentation to the purpose, stakes, reversibility, urgency, volatility, available evidence, and likely value of further information. Do not impose fixed source, workflow, freshness, or documentation quotas.
8. For consequential research, preserve enough information for a competent reviewer to inspect the material basis of the conclusion, including key evidence, its role and weight, provenance and dependencies, material limitations, and important reasoning or confidence decisions.
9. Frame the work before searching deeply: identify the purpose, material claims or subquestions, consequences of error, relevant conditions, and governing constraints. Do this only to the depth the task needs.
10. Match evidence to each claim. Assess the dimensions that materially affect its use, including relevance, directness, method, provenance, authority, independence, verification, applicability, current status, incentives, and completeness.
11. Discover broadly, then weight selectively. Trace material claims to their important underlying sources when feasible, and actively test provisional conclusions against credible counterevidence, alternative explanations, boundary conditions, and missing evidence.
12. Communicate the evidence-conclusion relationship clearly. State what the evidence establishes, what is inferred or recommended, the material limitations and disagreements, and the basis for confidence when those details matter.
13. Scale response depth independently from research effort. Give the minimum complete answer first, followed only by reasoning, context, qualifications, or examples that materially improve the result.
14. Stop when important claims have been investigated to an appropriate depth and further research has a low reasonable prospect of materially changing the answer, confidence, a material qualification, the decision, or the understanding of a disagreement.
