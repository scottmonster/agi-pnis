# General Research Guidance

This guidance supports rigorous, context-appropriate research across domains. Its core principles and guardrails apply to material research. Its methods, records, and output detail should be scaled to the question, stakes, time available, and governing requirements.

## Purpose

Use this guidance to find, evaluate, select, combine, and communicate evidence without assuming that one source type, method, or procedure is always best.

The central principle is **claim-evidence fit**: evidence is useful to the extent that it can responsibly support the specific claim being investigated for the specific research purpose. Clear provenance, sound and inspectable methods, relevant authority, independent verification, and corroboration generally deserve greater weight when they reduce uncertainty for that claim. They are not universal source rankings.

Evidence that lacks one or more of those qualities may still be useful when it is relevant or direct evidence for the question. Its limits should remain visible and should constrain the conclusion.

## How to Read This Guidance

_MUST_ and _MUST NOT_ identify requirements intrinsic to this guidance. The remaining practices, considerations, examples, and methods are advisory and should be adapted to the task. A requirement constrains only the aspect it states.

In this guidance, **material** describes a claim, uncertainty, or choice that could materially change the answer, confidence, an important qualification, a decision, or the understanding of an important disagreement. **Consequential** research is material research for which error, opacity, or unsupported confidence could have significant practical effects. These are context-dependent judgments, not fixed thresholds or scores.

## Normative Guardrails

### Claim-evidence fit

**Required:** Material evidence-selection, weighting, and conclusion decisions `MUST` be based on what the evidence can responsibly support for the claim and purpose at hand. A material distinction between directly established information and inference, estimation, forecast, recommendation, or value judgment `MUST` remain intelligible.

**Prohibited:** Research `MUST NOT` treat source type, institutional prestige, a pre-approved list, source count, numerical scoring, majority vote, repeated reporting, or confident wording as a substitute for claim-level assessment. It `MUST NOT` present inference, estimation, forecast, recommendation, or value judgment as directly established fact, or imply greater certainty or generality than the evidence supports. Evidence `MUST NOT` be excluded solely because it is anecdotal, subjective, preliminary, disputed, self-interested, informal, or unverified when it can materially inform the claim.

**Why:** These boundaries preserve claim-evidence fit while preventing both overstatement and the exclusion of relevant but limited evidence.

### Independence and corroboration

**Required:** When independence or corroboration materially affects confidence in a claim, evidence that shares a material underlying information path `MUST` be treated as dependent to that extent.

**Prohibited:** Duplicated, derivative, syndicated, or materially common-source evidence `MUST NOT` be counted as independent corroboration merely because it appears in multiple places.

**Why:** Repetition does not create independent support.

### Material limitations and incomplete research

**Required:** Identified uncertainty, contradiction, missing evidence, dependency, correction, retraction, supersession, staleness, conflict of interest, or other limitation that could materially change a conclusion's interpretation or confidence `MUST` be reflected in that conclusion or its qualification. If time, access, or available evidence prevents a sufficiently supported conclusion, the result `MUST` communicate the best-supported partial conclusion and remaining decision-relevant uncertainty.

**Prohibited:** Research `MUST NOT` conceal or neutralize material limitations or uncertainty merely to create a cleaner conclusion or manufacture closure.

**Why:** Research should reduce avoidable uncertainty without hiding uncertainty that remains.

### Proportionality

**Required:** Evidence thresholds, research depth, verification, freshness checking, and documentation `MUST` remain proportionate to the purpose, stakes, reversibility, urgency, volatility, available evidence, and likely value of further information when those factors materially affect the work.

**Prohibited:** This guidance `MUST NOT` be applied as a universal evidence threshold, source quota, freshness rule, research-depth quota, identical workflow, or documentation burden regardless of the task.

**Why:** The appropriate level of rigor depends on the research question and its consequences.

### Auditability

**Required:** Consequential research `MUST` preserve enough information for a competent reviewer to understand the material basis of its conclusions, including why key evidence received its role and weight and what important limitations affected the result.

**Prohibited:** A consequential conclusion `MUST NOT` depend materially on an evidentiary or reasoning chain that cannot be inspected to the degree reasonably necessary for the task without making that limitation visible.

**Why:** Inspectability lets a reviewer evaluate whether the conclusion follows from the evidence.

### Governing authority and domain methods

**Required:** Applicable binding requirements and task constraints `MUST` govern where they conflict with this general guidance.

**Prohibited:** This general guidance `MUST NOT` be used to disregard an applicable validated domain-specific method whose assumptions fit the work.

**Why:** A general framework cannot replace requirements or methods that are more authoritative or more suitable for the specific question.

## Core Principles

### Evaluate evidence in relation to the claim

Ask what the evidence can establish for the claim at hand. The same source may be direct evidence for one claim, indirect evidence for another, useful context for a third, and unsuitable for a fourth.

Distinguish what a source directly establishes from what is inferred, estimated, forecast, recommended, or valued. A recommendation combines evidence with objectives, constraints, values, and risk tolerance; evidence does not resolve those considerations by itself.

### Prefer stronger evidence without excluding relevant evidence

When practical and useful, seek evidence with clear provenance, inspectable methods, relevant authority, current status, and independent verification. Relevant imperfect evidence may still be useful, but its limitations should constrain the conclusion.

For example, a first-person account can directly establish that a person reports an experience. It normally cannot establish how common that experience is. A company statement can establish what the company says or officially does, but may require independent support for comparative safety, effectiveness, or customer-satisfaction claims.

### Preserve material uncertainty and disagreement

Research should reduce avoidable uncertainty. Preserve uncertainty that remains when it materially affects the conclusion.

## Frame the Research

Before searching deeply, identify enough of the situation to choose a proportionate approach:

- the decision, explanation, comparison, forecast, or understanding the research will support;
- the material claims or subquestions;
- the consequences of error, reversibility, urgency, and volatility;
- whether the topic is sparse, emerging, contested, proprietary, or subject to adversarial manipulation; and
- governing law, regulation, professional standards, organizational rules, or task constraints.

These considerations guide judgment. They are not a ritual that every task must document in full.

## Match Evidence to the Claim

Different parts of one research question can require different evidence. Use evidence appropriate to each claim rather than forcing all claims through one standard.

| Claim or question | Evidence qualities often especially useful | Common limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Current fact, event, status, or record | Direct records or observation, clear provenance, current status | Repeated reports can have one origin. |
| Causation, safety, effectiveness, or performance | Claim-appropriate methods, relevant comparison, applicable conditions, reproducibility or independent testing | Prestige or sample size cannot repair an unsuitable design. |
| Law, regulation, policy, or institutional status | Current controlling text, correct jurisdiction, authoritative interpretation where needed | Commentary explains but does not replace the controlling source. |
| Estimate or quantitative description | Transparent definitions, measurement, sampling, uncertainty, and applicability | Precise numbers can still be biased or inapplicable. |
| Forecast or scenario | Transparent assumptions, model structure, validation or calibration where possible, alternatives and sensitivity | Forecast confidence is not certainty that an event will occur. |
| Experience, perception, preference, trust, adoption, or social meaning | Testimony, interviews, qualitative research, surveys, reviews, community discussion, or behavior evidence as appropriate | Individual experience does not by itself establish prevalence or representativeness. |
| Recommendation or choice | Evidence about outcomes plus goals, values, constraints, feasibility, costs, risks, and trade-offs | Evidence does not resolve the values that determine the choice. |

For sentiment and lived experience, keep four questions separate when relevant:

- **Existence:** Does this view or experience occur?
- **Prevalence:** How common is it?
- **Distribution:** Which groups hold or experience it?
- **Meaning:** How do people understand or explain it?

An anecdote can be strong evidence for existence or meaning while being weak evidence for prevalence or distribution.

## Find and Appraise Evidence

### Discover broadly, then weight selectively

Use broad discovery to learn terminology, find original material, expose competing explanations, identify affected groups, and locate domain-specific methods. Potentially useful channels include records, datasets, research, syntheses, expert analysis, reporting, interviews, surveys, community discussion, organizational statements, marketing, and disputed or adversarial material.

Discovery and evidentiary weight are separate decisions. A weak item can reveal a useful claim, counterclaim, lead, or underlying source without deserving significant weight in the conclusion.

### Trace evidence lineage

For material claims, identify what each visible source ultimately depends on. Several reports may derive from the same study, dataset, press release, witness, event, wire story, model, or analyst estimate.

Treat dependent items as an evidence family, not automatically as independent corroboration. Record meaningful partial dependence where it affects the conclusion. Two analyses using the same dataset may contribute distinct interpretation, but they are not two independent observations of the underlying phenomenon.

Trace important claims toward an inspectable origin when feasible. Avoid assuming an original source is always best: a high-quality synthesis can be more suitable for a broad question, and an independent investigation can reveal defects in a self-interested primary source.

### Examine the dimensions that matter

Avoid reducing evidence to a universal score. Consider the relevant dimensions and explain the ones that materially influence the conclusion:

- **Relevance and directness:** Does it address the actual claim, population, setting, period, and outcome, or only a proxy?
- **Method and reliability:** Was it generated using a method suited to the claim? Could sampling, measurement, confounding, missingness, selection, or assumptions materially mislead?
- **Provenance and inspectability:** Can its origin and important transformations be traced and checked to the degree the task requires?
- **Independence and corroboration:** Do apparently agreeing sources arise from separate observations or information paths?
- **Verification status:** Has the item been authenticated, reproduced, cross-checked, or independently investigated? What remains uncertain if not?
- **Authority and expertise:** Does the source have authority or expertise relevant to this particular claim?
- **Representativeness and applicability:** Who or what does the evidence represent, and does that match the intended population or setting?
- **Temporal fitness and status:** Is it current enough, and has it been corrected, updated, superseded, withdrawn, or retracted?
- **Incentives and conflicts:** Could financial, political, legal, reputational, advocacy, or other interests affect collection, disclosure, analysis, or framing?
- **Completeness:** What relevant evidence is unavailable, inaccessible, unpublished, deleted, or otherwise missing?

A conflict of interest is not automatic disqualification. It is a reason to inspect the evidence more carefully and seek independent support when that would materially change confidence.

### Challenge the emerging answer

When a provisional conclusion forms, actively consider credible counterevidence, alternative explanations, different methods or information channels, corrections, relevant subgroups, boundary conditions, and missing evidence that would be expected if the conclusion were wrong.

Avoid artificial balance. Give contrary evidence weight according to its claim-fit and limitations, not merely because it disagrees.

## Synthesize and Communicate

For material conclusions, make the relationship between evidence and conclusion intelligible. Depending on what is material to the claim, this may include what the evidence directly establishes; what is inferred, estimated, forecast, or recommended; important dependencies; material contradictions or limitations; relevant unknowns; and the basis for stated confidence.

Scale response depth independently from research effort. Start with the minimum complete answer that satisfies the user's request. Add reasoning, context, qualifications, examples, or detail only when they materially improve correctness, clarity, safety, actionability, or understanding. Do not make the response longer merely because the research involved many sources, steps, or considerations. Prefer the answer first, followed by only the explanation and nuance needed to support it.

Do not include citations, source lists, source links, or other source references in a user-facing response unless the user explicitly asks for citations, sources, or where information or a claim came from. This applies equally when the response is written to a file. This does not limit keeping the research records needed for auditability.

Confidence belongs to a claim in context, not permanently to a source. It generally increases with direct, fit-for-purpose, current, representative, independent, and well-verified evidence. It decreases with indirectness, dependency, poor fit, unclear provenance, material conflict, stale conditions, selective observation, and untested assumptions.

Use plain confidence language when it helps the reader, and explain its basis. Avoid converting uncertainty into arithmetic or false precision. Use a domain's established confidence vocabulary when it is applicable.

## Scale Effort and Decide When to Stop

Research depth, verification burden, freshness checks, and documentation should be proportionate to the stakes, reversibility, urgency, volatility, and likely value of further information.

Evidence may be sufficient when important claims have been investigated to an appropriate depth and further research has a low reasonable prospect of materially changing the answer, confidence, an important qualification, the decision, or the understanding of a material disagreement.

Further research is especially worthwhile when a decisive claim rests on one uncertain lineage, credible contradictions remain unexplained, a material source's status is unclear, the evidence is too indirect, important populations or conditions are absent, or new evidence could realistically change the decision.

If time or access ends before evidence is sufficient, report the best-supported partial conclusion and the remaining decision-relevant uncertainty. Avoid manufacturing closure.

## Keep Material Research Auditable

Auditability means a reader can understand where consequential conclusions came from and why the evidence received the role and weight it did. Keep records lightweight for simple work and more detailed for consequential work.

Records that often make consequential research auditable include:

- the question and important scope choices;
- the evidence used and what each item directly establishes;
- provenance, source lineage, and important dependencies;
- material limitations, conflicts, corrections, status, and missing evidence;
- key reasoning, confidence, and stopping decisions; and
- important exclusions, inaccessible sources, or time limits that affect the result.

Before finalizing consequential research, check the relevant questions:

- If citations were requested, does each citation support the claim for which it is used?
- Have dependent sources been distinguished from independent corroboration?
- Are material corrections, retractions, supersession, and freshness concerns addressed?
- Are facts, reported statements, inference, estimates, forecasts, opinions, and recommendations distinguishable?
- Are informal or subjective sources used for claims they can support, without treating them as representative without basis?
- Are conflicts, counterevidence, material disagreement, and uncertainty visible?
- Does the stated confidence match the evidence and remaining uncertainty?
- Was the research effort proportionate, and would further research likely change something material?

These checks protect the framework's core principles. They do not require a fixed template or exhaustive documentation.

## Domain-Specific Methods

Use stronger domain-specific methods when they apply. Law, medicine, safety, engineering, cybersecurity, public opinion, history, and forecasting can have specialized standards that are more appropriate than general guidance for particular questions.

General guidance provides common safeguards where no more specific method governs.

## Trade-offs and Reconsideration

This approach accepts real trade-offs:

- More provenance and traceability improve auditability but add time and overhead.
- Broad evidence discovery reduces blind spots but increases noise and manipulation risk.
- Common principles improve consistency, while domain-specific methods preserve accuracy and relevance.
- Confidence language improves communication but can oversimplify multidimensional uncertainty.
- Further research can improve a decision, but can also delay action without changing it.

Reconsider or supplement the framework if experience shows systematic overreliance on prestige, false corroboration, missed corrections, inappropriate exclusion or overgeneralization of experiential evidence, poorly calibrated confidence, hidden disagreement, excessive overhead, or conflict with a binding domain method.
